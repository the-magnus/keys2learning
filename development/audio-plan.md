# Audio plan: Building with agents and science

A working plan for making the program feel like a whole when heard as an audiobook. It builds on the format design notes from the 2026-11-18 audio session and a review of the first scripted draft (the prologue and chapter one, with Erik). Expect to iterate on it.

## Starting point (from the format design notes)

- The first live audio run took 24 min 40 s for all seven modules. It was accurate but disconnected: there was no thread from one module to the next, and it was cluttered with signposts, long lists, buried stories and "next is X" endings.
- The agreed direction: a thread across the program, with each module ending on the question the next one answers. Each format has its own job: the book for reference, audio for curiosity and the whole, interactive for recall and reasoning, desk work for building. The workbook is the hinge between formats. Each concept has layers (hook, claim, evidence, ear and page versions, a reasoning question). The running case is a stock case first, then the learner's own system. Evidence marks get lighter in audio, never absent.
- Still open: should the formats be a path or a menu?

## What the scripted draft got right (keep)

1. Each chapter grows out of the previous chapter's question ("Forty pieces of work, and only one Erik").
2. Evidence is promised once, then marked lightly; "my reading" stands for the tutor's own links.
3. Chapters open on a story (the children's party) before naming the concept.

## Changes to make

1. **Erik acts and gets things wrong.** Open each chapter on a mistake of Erik's, such as the email agent sending 200 polished emails and getting two replies, then bring in the thinker to explain it. Borrowed stories (the party, the kitchen) come second.
2. **Set up the payoff early.** Chapter seven ends on "the constraint may be you." The prologue should plant a detail that sets this up: Erik reviews agent output at midnight, or he is the only one who approves anything.
3. **No list of seven in the prologue.** Name the first question and promise that each answer raises the next.
4. **Episodes, not modules, as the unit.** Each episode runs 8–10 minutes, one bus ride. Split chapter one after "Erik has been treating it like a plan."
5. **One silent pause per episode.** A prompt to think about the learner's own work, with no answer expected. It engages the listener without questions and prepares the desk session.
6. **A 20-second "previously" at the start of each episode**, to rebuild the thread after a day away.
7. **End on the open question itself**, not "That's chapter two."
8. **Write for the ear.** Give each person one handle on first mention ("Snowden, who studies how organisations decide"), then use the surname only. Keep to about two new names per episode. Spell out numbers. Put quotes behind "they wrote." Never read out more than three list items; otherwise give examples and say "eight in all."

## Where it lives

- **Recommended:** written audio scripts in the program, for example `programs/building-with-agents-and-science/audio.md`, with one section per episode. The live improvised run drifted into "textbook read aloud," while the scripted draft worked. A script can be reviewed and plays the same for every learner. Erik is fictional, so he fits the rule that program files speak to any learner.
- After the first desk session, the tutor may swap in the learner's own case live.

## Path or menu

- **Recommendation:** the tutor recommends the path (hear, recall, build, conclude) and adapts when the learner asks for something else, as it did on the bus. Keep treating this as a probe.

## Tutor reliability (for `tutoring.md`)

- In the session the tutor claimed saves it likely couldn't make ("saved your workbook to the project", then "can't update on this call", then "updated", then "saved the prologue to the project"). The tutor should say plainly what it can and can't save in each setting, and never claim a save it hasn't made.

## Module order (under discussion)

- **Leaning: swap modules 1 and 2.** "What is worth doing?" comes first, because you diagnose before you sort. Cynefin then sorts the work that serves the crux, and the hand-off becomes "now I know what's worth doing, but some of it can be planned and some only probed."
- **"What is worth doing" needs more material.** It rests on Rumelt and Rother, both theory. Candidates to check:
  - Locke and Latham, goal-setting theory. Large empirical base and meta-analyses: specific, hard goals beat "do your best." Ordóñez and colleagues' "Goals gone wild" is the counterpoint on narrow goals.
  - Kahneman and Lovallo, and Flyvbjerg: the outside view and reference-class forecasting. Judge worth with base rates, not the inside story.
  - Gollwitzer and Sheeran, implementation intentions. A meta-analysis on turning a chosen goal into action.
  - Reinertsen, cost of delay (practice). Worth includes when.
  - Teresa Torres, the opportunity solution tree (practice), and Annie Duke on quitting (practice).
  - Products that make people awesome. Kathy Sierra's idea (*Badass: Making Users Awesome*), which Modern Agile took up as "make people awesome." Practice. Worth is judged by what the product makes its users able to do, not by the product's own place at the centre.
  - The counterpoint, as the author's view and labelled as such: a money-first perspective bloats promising products and makes them want to be the centrepiece, instead of supporting with a light touch only where needed. Cory Doctorow's "enshittification" names a close cousin (essay, practice).
- **Other points still open:** agents arrive late (4 of 7), so either move them up or keep Erik's agents on stage throughout. Sarasvathy is split between 1 and 7. The constraint appears in 3, 4 and 7: make it a deliberate build-up or say it once. Seeing (6) may belong nearer 3.

## The bug example (fix)

- "A bug with a known cause is complicated" is true only sometimes, and it makes engineers cringe. An intermittent bug with several underlying causes and race conditions starts in the complex domain. A skilled engineer probes with many quick hypotheses to narrow down the area. Once it is found, the problem becomes complicated: understood, analysed, fixed, and the fix tested.
- Use this as the better example: a single piece of work moves between domains as understanding grows. This is Snowden's own point that the domain belongs to a part of the work at a moment, not to a category of task. It also shows probes and analysis in sequence, not as rivals.
- Use the full range under one label. A misspelled heading is a clear bug: the problem and the fix are both obvious, so you sense, categorise, respond. A bug with a known cause is complicated: sense, analyse, respond. An intermittent race condition is complex: probe, sense, respond. All three are called "bug," but each is handled differently. The lesson is that the name of the task doesn't tell you the domain.
- The emails make a second example of the same kind. A non-reply can't be traced back to a cause: the email may have been perfect and still sit under 300 unread ones, or it may have been wildly off. The outcome is shaped by causes outside the sender's reach, so reading a single silence as a verdict, or taking it personally, mistakes complex work for an ordered one. Only a pattern across several probes says anything. A possible Erik scene: he rewrites a good email after one silence.
- Check the textbook and the Erik draft for other examples that put a task type in one domain for good.
