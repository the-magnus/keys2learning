# Audio plan: Building with agents and science

A working plan for an audiobook version of the program: one that feels like a whole, makes the listener curious, and stays evidenced. It builds on the 2026-11-18 audio session, its format design notes, a review of the first scripted draft (a prologue and chapter one, with a made-up builder called Erik), and a second opinion from a separate agent on an earlier version of this plan. Expect to iterate on it.

Words used here: a **module** is a part of the textbook; a **chapter** is the same part told for the ear; an **episode** is one listening, about one bus ride.

## 1. The aim

1. **Audio's job is to make the desk session wanted,** not to land points. Points land when the learner reasons on their own work, which audio on a bus can't ask for. Audio gives curiosity, the whole picture, and a reason to come back.
2. **Story carries, evidence anchors.** The listener follows a person and a question. Each idea still says what stands behind it, lightly, and never disappears.
3. **One question runs through it all.** Erik's need, "is any of it working?", is asked in the prologue and answered at the end. Every chapter ends on the question the next one answers.
4. **The answer rests on evidence, not on the story.** METR's trial is Erik's question measured: developers can't tell by feel whether AI helps them, so only fast, clear feedback can tell (textbook module 4). The ending says so, with the trial's limits audible: sixteen developers, early-2025 models.

## 2. Where we start

1. **The live run** (24 min 40 s, all seven modules) was accurate but disconnected. It was cluttered with signposts ("the tutor's link", "on the evidence", progress lines), it read long lists aloud, it buried its stories mid-part, it ended each part with "next is X", and it gave the program's map only at the end.
2. **The scripted draft** of the prologue and chapter one was much better. Keep from it:
   1. Each chapter grows out of the last one's question ("Forty pieces of work, and only one Erik").
   2. Evidence promised once, then marked lightly; "my reading" stands for the tutor's own links.
   3. Opening on a story (the children's party) before naming the concept.
3. **The draft is not in the repository yet.** It lives in the Claude Project where it was written.

## 3. The story spine

1. **Erik is the running case,** introduced as made up. He has a target, three agents (code, review, customer emails) and the nagging feeling that he can't tell whether any of it works.
2. **Erik acts, and is sometimes wrong.** Many chapters open on a scene where Erik makes a mistake that the chapter's ideas explain. Example: his email agent sends 200 polished emails and gets two replies, because he planned work that could only be probed. But not every time: sometimes Erik gets it right without knowing why, and the idea names what he did. If he is always wrong he becomes a straw man.
3. **Erik's agents are on stage in every chapter,** not only in "Agents as team members". The program is about building with agents, and a listener can't skim ahead to where they appear.
4. **One arc across the series: Erik's attention.** Plant it in the prologue (he reviews agent output at midnight; nothing ships until he approves it). It shows as a strain in "The road", where the constraint is first named; gets its name in "Agents as team members"; and is resolved in "Staying well in it" ("the constraint may be you"). This turns the constraint's three appearances from repetition into the series' arc. **Every beat of this arc is spoken as "my reading":** "your attention is the constraint" and "the pile forms at review" are the tutor's links in the textbook, not the sources' claims, so the backbone of the story is also its least evidenced part.
5. **Callbacks, not repeats.** An idea is introduced once and later recalled by its image. Sarasvathy's kitchen comes early, beside the probes; "Staying well in it" returns with "remember the cupboards" and adds only what is new (lemonade, the crazy quilt).
6. **Felt need before the answer.** Where a chapter answers something earlier chapters needed, show Erik needing it first. Example: in "The road" and "Agents as team members" Erik can't see where work piles up or what an agent is doing, so "Seeing it at a glance" answers a need already felt.
7. **Erik stays Erik in the audio.** The learner's own system belongs to the interactive and desk formats, where the tutor works with it live. Putting it into the audio would bring back the improvising that section 8 rejects.

## 4. Evidence in the ear

1. **The promise, once.** The prologue says that ideas stand on different ground (trials, frameworks, advice from experience), that each will be named lightly, and that "my reading" marks the narrator's own links. It can also carry, in a sentence, the idea of grading evidence, if the swap in 7.1 moves that concept later.
2. **A light spoken mark for each kind,** worked into the sentence, never a separate line: "a review of many studies", "a randomised trial", "a survey", "a benchmark", "advice from experience", and for theory, "a framework, a claim still waiting to be tested". Most of the program is theory or practice, so "a framework" must not sound like evidence.
3. **Evidence as plot.** The strongest findings make the best turns of the story: METR's developers were slower with AI but believed they were faster; Dybå and Dingsøyr found agile taken whole stands on very low evidence. Place them where they surprise. Some episodes can open on the finding instead of on a scene.
4. **The story mustn't feel like proof.** A scene written so that the theory explains it confirms the theory by construction. Erik's scenes are fiction and never sound like a finding. At least once, an idea fails Erik or leaves him unsure, and the narration says what isn't known. When the evidence is thin, say so in the story ("nobody has tested this yet").
5. **Only confirmed stories about thinkers.** Story first tempts invention. A story about a real person goes in only if the textbook or a checked source has it; the tutor's improvised stories were cut before for this reason (see `notes.md`).
6. **Show notes for each episode,** with its sources, their kinds of evidence, and the full lists the audio only sampled, so a listener can check what they heard.

## 5. Episode craft

1. **Length is measured, not estimated.** The scripted chapter one covered all eight concepts of module 1 in about 1,100 words, roughly seven or eight minutes, which suggests a chapter is about one episode and the book about one to one and a half hours. Measure the redrafted chapter one, and set the pace and total from that.
2. **Choose what the ear gets.** Each chapter carries its module's Must cover points and one strong image; the rest stays in the textbook and show notes. Diagrams that can't be followed by ear (Dettmer's trees, the eight reservation categories) are named and described in a sentence, and left for the desk.
3. **Vary the template.** The pieces below are tools, not a form for every episode: the opening may be a scene, a thinker's story or a finding; a pause comes where it fits, not always in the same place.
4. **A short "previously"** opens an episode after a break, to rebuild the thread.
5. **A pause to think,** with a spoken cue around it ("take a moment… and back"), since on a bus silence sounds like a dropout. It asks the listener to think of their own work, with no answer expected. A reply read aloud can't hold silence, so the reply ends at the question and the listener's "continue" ends the pause (heard in the first listen: an ellipsis is read as no pause at all).
6. **End on the open question itself,** left hanging. Not "That's chapter two", not "next is X".
7. **The prologue doesn't list the seven questions.** It names the first and promises that each answer raises the next.
8. **A closing recap of the series** pays off the arc: Erik's attention, the question from the prologue, and how each chapter answered the last one.
9. **Same word, two meanings:** Snowden's constraints come before Goldratt's. At Goldratt's first mention the narration says they are different things (textbook module 1, Must cover 3).
10. **Write for the ear:**
    1. Each person gets one handle at first mention ("Snowden, who studies how organisations decide"), then the surname only. About two new names per episode.
    2. At most three items in a list; otherwise give examples and say "eight in all".
    3. Numbers spelled out; quotes set behind "they wrote".
    4. No markdown, brackets or abbreviations in the script: it is read aloud as written.
    5. Progress only at the start and end of a sitting, in one line.

## 6. Examples to fix

1. **The bug, as one label across the domains.** A misspelled heading is clear (sense, categorise, respond). A bug with a known cause is complicated (sense, analyse, respond). An intermittent race condition with several interacting causes starts complex (probe, sense, respond). All are called "bug"; the name of a task doesn't tell you its domain.
2. **Why the race condition starts complex.** Not because the person working on it lacks expertise: a novice facing complicated work needs an expert, not probes. It is complex at first because it can't yet be reproduced, so even an expert can't analyse it and must probe with hypotheses; experience only makes the probing quicker. Once it shows up on demand, it is complicated for everyone. Cynefin is a sense-making framework, so the domain describes the situation as people meet it in their concrete system, humans and agents included.
3. **A redesign can move the work for good.** Changing the design so that the race can't happen changes the constraints, which takes the work out of the complex domain altogether. This ties the example to module 1's constraints. Draft line for the ear: "At first nobody can reproduce it, so even the expert has to probe. Once it shows up on demand, it's complicated. And the best fix may be a design that makes the race impossible, which moves the work out of complex for good."
4. **The emails, the clean complex case.** A non-reply can't be traced back to a cause: a perfect email may sit under 300 unread ones, or the email may have been wildly off. Reading one silence as a verdict, or taking it personally, treats complex work as ordered; only a pattern across several probes says anything. Erik scene: he rewrites a good email after one silence.
5. **Textbook first.** "Work moves between domains as understanding grows" and "a redesign can move work out of complex" are not in the textbook yet. By section 8.2 they go there first, with a source (Kurtz and Snowden, 2003, discuss movement between domains; to check).
6. **Check the textbook and the draft** for other examples that pin a kind of task to one domain.

## 7. Module order and content

1. **Swap modules 1 and 2 (leaning yes).** "What is worth doing?" comes first: Erik asks whether any of it is working, and "working toward what?" is the natural next question. Cynefin then sorts the work that serves the crux, and the hand-off becomes "now I know what's worth doing, but some of it can be planned and some only probed." Sarasvathy's goals-from-means then lands as a turn against Rumelt. **Needed before deciding:** a building reason, not only an ear reason, since `about.md` says "the order is the one the building needs".
2. **"What is worth doing" needs evidence,** since it rests on Rumelt and Rother, both theory. As an opener it takes **one** new source, to respect the two-names rule; the leading candidate is the outside view (Kahneman and Lovallo; Flyvbjerg), which judges worth by base rates and ties to METR. Other candidates, for the textbook or later in the chapter, to check against the sources before use:
   1. Locke and Latham, goal-setting theory: decades of studies and meta-analyses; specific, hard goals beat "do your best", in tension with Rumelt's "a goal is not a strategy". Ordóñez and colleagues' "Goals gone wild" is the counterpoint on narrow goals.
   2. Gollwitzer and Sheeran: a meta-analysis on implementation intentions; a bridge to "The road".
   3. Reinertsen, cost of delay (practice): worth includes when.
   4. Torres's opportunity solution tree and Duke on quitting (practice): deciding what not to do, like Rumelt's policy that rules things out.
   5. Products that make people awesome: Kathy Sierra (*Badass: Making Users Awesome*), taken up by Modern Agile as "make people awesome" (practice).
   6. Not in the opener: the author's view that money-first thinking bloats promising products and makes them want to be the centrepiece, instead of supporting with a light touch where needed; and Doctorow's "enshittification" as its cousin.
3. **Agents stay in module 4, as now.** They are on stage throughout instead (3.3). Revisit if a listener still finds them late.
4. **Seeing stays in module 6.** The need for it is shown first (3.6). Revisit only if a listener says otherwise.
5. **What a swap changes:** the textbook's numbered cross-references ("module 1, concept 8" and the references in modules 4, 5 and 7 and in the Must cover points), `about.md`, the prologue and the progress lines. Either renumber them all at once, or refer to chapters by title in audio and decide the textbook order separately.

## 8. Where it lives

1. **Written audio scripts in the program,** for example `programs/building-with-agents-and-science/audio.md`, one section per episode, with its show notes. The improvised run drifted into a textbook read aloud; the scripted draft worked. A script can be reviewed, checked against the textbook's sources, and plays the same for every learner. Erik is made up, so he fits the rule that program files speak to any learner.
2. **The textbook stays the source of truth.** The script draws on it and adds no claim of its own; a new claim goes into the textbook first, with its source.

## 9. The tutor (for `tutoring.md`)

1. **Path or menu:** the tutor recommends the path (hear, recall, build, conclude) and adapts when the learner asks for something else, as it did on the bus. Treat this as a probe.
2. **Saves:** in the session the tutor claimed saves it likely couldn't make ("saved your workbook to the project", then "can't update on this call", then "updated", then "saved the prologue to the project"). The tutor should say plainly what it can save in each setting, and never claim a save it hasn't made.
