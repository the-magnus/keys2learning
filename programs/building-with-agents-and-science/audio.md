# Building with agents and science: the audiobook

The program told for the ear, in episodes of about one bus ride. The textbook is the source of truth: the scripts add no claim of their own, and every source is in the show notes. Erik is made up.

For the tutor: read an episode's script aloud as written, without adding headings, lists or progress lines inside it. A reply read aloud can't hold a silence, so end the reply after each "Take a moment" question, and go on from "And back" when the learner says so: the pause is theirs to end. Between episodes, name the next one and nothing more ("say continue for episode three"); progress is for the start and end of a sitting, and a workbook that can't be saved is said once. Offer the show notes when the learner is back at a screen. Listening makes the desk session wanted; it doesn't land points.

## Prologue

### Script

Picture a builder. Let's call him Erik. He's made up, but you may recognise him.

Erik has a target: a small product, launched by spring. He has three AI agents. One writes code, one reviews it, and one drafts emails to his first customers. The agents are fast, and the tokens are flowing. Every morning Erik opens his laptop to more work than the day before. And every night, around midnight, he's still there, reading what the agents made, because nothing ships until he has said yes.

Erik has a quiet, nagging feeling. He can't say whether any of it is working.

This book follows that feeling. It asks one question at a time, and each answer raises the next one. The first is the plainest: working toward what?

A word about who's talking. I'm the tutor for this program, an AI. I didn't do this research. I've read it, and I'll tell you how sure each part is. Some ideas here were tested in randomised trials, which can show cause. Some come from surveys, which show that two things go together. Many are frameworks: careful claims from researchers, still waiting to be tested. And some are advice from people who've done the work. I'll name which, lightly, as we go. When I join two ideas myself, I'll say "my reading".

So. Erik, at his laptop, with forty pieces of open work. Where should he start?

### Show notes

1. Erik and his system are fiction, there to show the ideas at work; nothing about him is evidence.
2. The kinds of evidence, as the textbook uses them: review, trial, study, benchmark, theory, practice, summary (textbook, introduction).

## Episode 1: Working toward what?

### Script

Erik's target is clear, he thinks. Launch by spring, a hundred paying customers. He's written it at the top of his notes. Under it are forty pieces of work, and he does a bit of each, every day.

Here's the trouble. A hundred customers by spring is a goal. It says where Erik wants to be. It doesn't say how he'll get there, or what he'll stop doing.

Richard Rumelt, who studies strategy, has a blunt name for this. He calls it bad strategy. Not because the goal is wrong, but because a goal mistaken for a strategy leaves every one of those forty pieces looking equally worth doing.

What does good strategy look like? Rumelt says it has a kernel, with three parts. First, a diagnosis: what's really going on, simplified down to what's critical. Second, a guiding policy: an overall approach that rules things out. A policy that rules nothing out isn't guiding anything. Third, coherent actions: steps that reinforce each other, not a list of activities that just sit side by side.

The diagnosis comes first, because everything rests on it. So Erik tries. What is really going on? After a long evening, he writes one sentence: "Nobody outside my head has seen this product yet." That changes things. His approach becomes: get it in front of real people before polishing anything. And suddenly, half of the forty pieces fall away. The new logo. The settings page. The second language.

Rumelt adds one more idea: the crux. Of all the challenges you face, the crux is the one that matters most and that you can actually do something about. Both at once. Something important but impossible isn't the crux. Neither is something easy but trivial. For Erik, the crux is that first contact with real users.

Rumelt's work is a framework, built from cases and long experience. It's a careful claim, not a tested result.

Now, Erik sets himself a date. A first version in front of ten people in three weeks. And here a different kind of evidence has something to say.

Daniel Kahneman, the psychologist, once helped a team write a new school curriculum. Early on, everyone wrote down how long they thought it would take. The answers ranged from eighteen months to thirty. Then the team's most experienced member was asked a different question: think of other teams who've done something like this. How long did they take? The expert went quiet. About forty percent of those teams, he said, never finished at all. And he couldn't think of any that finished in less than seven years.

The team went on anyway. They finished eight years later, and the curriculum was rarely used.

Kahneman and Dan Lovallo call the first kind of estimate the inside view. You look at your own plan, its details, its steps, and it always looks doable. The second is the outside view. You set your plan aside and ask how efforts like it actually went. Bent Flyvbjerg, who studies why big projects run over, turned that into a method: find a class of similar past projects, see how they spread out, and place yours among them. He finds the advantage is largest for work nobody in the room has done before. This is a framework too, though Flyvbjerg has put it to use on real infrastructure budgets.

So Erik asks the outside question. How long did other people's first versions take to reach ten real users? Not three weeks. More like two months. His date moves. His target doesn't.

Take a moment with that. Is there something in your own work that you've only ever judged from the inside?

And back to Erik.

There's one more piece. Mike Rother studied how Toyota improves, and he separates the challenge, the long direction, from the target condition, the next stop on the way. A target condition describes where you want to be at a date a week to a few months out. It sits just beyond what you currently know how to do, so you'll have to learn your way there. And it says where, not how. The how is found by trying. Rother's work is a framework as well.

So Erik has it now. A diagnosis, an approach that rules out half his list, a crux, and a next stop he can actually reach. Rumelt tells him where to aim. Rother tells him how to walk. The outside view tells him how long the walk really is.

He looks at what's left on the list. Twenty pieces. And he notices something odd about them. Some, he knows exactly how to do. Others, nobody could know in advance, however hard they thought about it.

Should he treat them the same way?

### Show notes

1. Rumelt, *Good Strategy Bad Strategy* (2011) and *The Crux* (2022) (theory): the kernel, bad strategy (fluff, not facing the challenge, goals mistaken for strategy, bad objectives), the crux, proximate objectives.
2. Lovallo and Kahneman (2003), "Delusions of Success", as told by Flyvbjerg (2006), "From Nobel Prize to Project Management: Getting Risks Right", *Project Management Journal* 37(3) (theory, with its first use in practice): the inside and outside view, reference-class forecasting in three steps, and the curriculum story.
3. Rother, *Toyota Kata* (2009) and the *Improvement Kata Handbook* (theory; practice): the challenge and the target condition.
4. Not in the episode: Rumelt's proximate objective and his reading of Kennedy's moon goal (textbook module 1, concept 4).

## Episode 2: Planned or probed?

### Script

Previously: Erik found what's worth doing, and cut his list in half. But the twenty pieces left aren't all alike.

His email agent sent a careful, polished message to a potential customer. A week passed. No reply. So Erik rewrote it. Shorter, friendlier, a better subject line. He was sure the first one had failed.

Had it? Maybe the email was wildly off. Or maybe it was perfect, and it's sitting under three hundred unread messages in an inbox belonging to someone who's having a terrible month. From one silence, Erik can't tell. Nobody could.

To see why, picture a children's party. Ten children, one living room, two hours. You could write a schedule: games at three, cake at half past. Anyone who has tried knows that by ten past three, the schedule is fiction.

Dave Snowden, who studies how organisations make decisions, uses this story to show what works instead. You draw a line in the sand: a boundary, flexible, open to negotiation. No running upstairs, say. Inside it, you set out a few things for the children to gather around. Then you watch. When a good game forms, you help it grow. When a game turns rough, you calm it down. You don't control the party. You steer what emerges.

Would you do your tax return like that? Of course not. And that's Snowden's central point: work differs in kind. In some work, cause and effect can be known in advance. You can study it, plan it, get it right. In other work, cause and effect only show up looking back. Afterwards, it all makes sense. Beforehand, nobody could have said.

His framework for this is called Cynefin, a Welsh word that roughly means habitat. It has four domains, plus a fifth state. Clear work has an obvious answer: you sense, categorise and respond. Complicated work has an answer that takes analysis or expertise to find: you sense, analyse, then respond. Complex work has no answer waiting: you probe, sense, and respond, like at the party. Chaos has no pattern at all, so you act first, just to get a footing. And when you don't know which one you're in, Snowden calls that confusion.

Now here's what Erik's email teaches. The reply depends on things outside his reach. That's complex work. Reading one silence as a verdict treats it as if it were ordered, as if the cause could be traced. Only a pattern across several emails says anything at all.

Take something more technical. A bug. Is a bug complicated? Sometimes. A misspelled heading is clear: you see it, you fix it. A bug whose cause you can trace is complicated: you analyse, then fix. But Erik's code agent has been chasing a bug that appears only now and then, from several causes interacting, a race condition. Nobody can make it happen on demand. So at first, not even an expert can analyse it. Everyone has to probe: try a hypothesis, watch, try another. Experience makes the probing quicker. It doesn't skip it. Then one day the bug shows up on demand, and from that moment it's complicated, for everyone.

All three are called a bug. The name of a task doesn't tell you its domain. And a piece of work can move between domains as you learn. Snowden's own materials say as much: making sense of a situation always has some fluidity, as the context changes and knowledge grows.

There's one more way to move work, and it's my favourite. Snowden says each domain has its own kind of constraint. Clear work has rigid ones, fixed rules. Complicated work has governing ones, limits like laws and codes that leave room inside them. Complex work has enabling ones, which give people the means to act and adapt; they tilt the system without deciding what it does. Remember the line in the sand. And Snowden's materials say that changing the constraints is one way to move work from one domain to another. So the best fix for Erik's race condition might be a design in which the race can't happen at all. That changes the constraints, and moves that work out of the complex for good.

Snowden's work is a framework, built from research and cases. A careful claim, still to be tested.

Take a moment. Is there something in your work that you've been planning, when it can only be probed?

And back.

Erik sorts his twenty pieces: planned here, probed there. But the probes bother him. If he can't plan them, how does he choose which ones to try?

### Show notes

1. Snowden: the Cynefin domains and their ways of deciding; constraints, rigid, governing, enabling, none; the children's party (Snowden and Stanbridge, 2004; Cynefin wiki); work moving between domains and constraints as a way to move it (Cynefin wiki, Cynefin dynamics) (theory; practice).
2. Snowden's "constraint" is not Goldratt's, heard in episode 4: in Cynefin constraints bias a system; in the Theory of Constraints the constraint sets a system's throughput.
3. The bug and the email examples are the textbook's illustrations, not findings.

## Episode 3: What's in the cupboard

### Script

Previously: Erik sorted his work into what can be planned and what can only be probed. Now he has to choose the probes.

Snowden calls them safe-to-fail probes: small experiments whose failure you can afford. What works, you amplify. What doesn't, you dampen. But which experiments?

Here a second thinker comes in. Saras Sarasvathy studied how expert entrepreneurs think, and her simplest example starts in the kitchen. One way to cook is to decide on the dish, then buy the ingredients. She calls that causal logic, and it fits when the future can be predicted. The other way is to open the cupboards, see what's there, and ask what you could make. She calls that effectual logic, and it fits when the future can't be predicted.

Erik's cupboard holds three agents, his own skills, and the four people who did reply to his emails. So his first probe isn't a big campaign. It's a call with those four. That's effectual. It starts from what he has.

Sarasvathy's work is a framework too, built from studying expert entrepreneurs.

Snowden makes the same turn from another side. Don't start from a distant goal, he says. Start from the present. Describe how things are now, and ask what could change from here. That gives you a direction to travel in rather than a destination. His phrase for it is: more stories like these, fewer like those. The label "start from the present" is the textbook's. The idea is his.

Now for some harder evidence. Suppose Erik skipped all this sorting and took up a popular agile framework, whole. It would come with sprints, stand-ups, a board, all of it ready made.

Two researchers, Tore Dybå and Torgeir Dingsøyr, went looking for the evidence behind agile methods taken as a whole. They reviewed thirty-six studies, and they graded the strength of that evidence as very low. Any estimate of effect, they wrote, is very uncertain.

This one isn't a framework. It's a systematic review, the strongest kind of source in this program. And it says that the bundle, as a whole, has very little behind it. Single practices are a different matter: they can be tested one at a time, and we'll meet some that have been. Snowden's own short phrase for agile is: good practice, poor theory. The practices often help. But nothing in the bundle tells you which kind of work they fit.

My reading: a framework adopted whole skips the very question Erik just answered.

One thing to carry from this. When you hear a claim, ask what stands behind it. A trial can show cause. A survey shows that things go together. A framework is a claim waiting for evidence. Advice is experience. Dybå and Dingsøyr used a grading scale for exactly this, and it's why every source in this book wears a label.

So Erik has his sorted list, his first probes, and no borrowed framework. For the first time in weeks, the pile has a shape.

But it's still a pile. Twenty pieces, and some of them can't start until others are done. Which one is he actually waiting on?

### Show notes

1. Snowden, safe-to-fail probes and the vector theory of change, "more stories like these, fewer like those" (Cynefin wiki) (theory; practice).
2. Sarasvathy (2001), "Causation and Effectuation", and her kitchen example in an interview (theory).
3. Dybå and Dingsøyr (2008), a systematic review of thirty-six empirical studies of agile development, evidence graded "very low" (review). Snowden's "good practice, poor theory", reported by InfoQ, 2012 (summary).
4. How evidence is graded, after the GRADE scale: high, moderate, low, very low; starting from the design and moved by quality, consistency and directness (textbook module 2, concept 8).

## Episode 4: Where the road waits

### Script

Previously: Erik knows what's worth doing, and which work to plan and which to probe. Now he has to put it in order.

His agents are fast. The code agent opens three changes a day. The review agent comments on all of them. The email agent drafts ten messages before breakfast. And every evening, a little more piles up in one place: in front of Erik.

Eliyahu Goldratt, a management thinker, wrote a novel about a factory, called The Goal. In it, a scout troop goes hiking. One boy, Herbie, is the slowest. However fast the others walk, the troop can only move at Herbie's pace. Goldratt's point is that a system's output is set by its constraint, its weakest link. Improving anything else doesn't raise it.

A word on that word. In the last chapter, Snowden's constraints were rules that shape what a system does. Goldratt's constraint is something else: the one place that sets the pace. Same word, two meanings.

Goldratt gives five focusing steps. Find the constraint. Get the most out of it as it is. Then line everything else up behind it. There are two more, about adding capacity and starting over, five in all. To find it, look for where work piles up. It isn't always visible. And it's often not a machine, but a policy: the way things have always been done.

My reading: when agents produce work fast, the pile tends to form at the person reviewing it. For Erik, that's the midnight reading.

Goldratt's work is a framework. Now, how do you lay out the road itself? H. William Dettmer, a practitioner who built on Goldratt, draws it as trees. In one, you list the obstacles to your objective, give each one an intermediate objective that gets past it, and put them in the order they depend on each other. You read it as: in order to do this, we must first do that. In another, you test a proposed change before making it, reading its effects forward, if this, then that, until you spot the unwanted ones. He calls those negative branches.

Trees are hard to follow by ear, so they're in the show notes, and at the desk. But here's the part that matters for Erik. My reading again: an ordered list of objectives is a plan an agent can follow and report against. And an agent can be asked, before a step is taken, to read it forward and name what could go wrong.

Dettmer's trees are practice: a method from experience.

Erik tries it. He asks his code agent to read the next change forward. It names one negative branch he hadn't seen: the change would break the email links. Small, but it would have cost him an evening.

Take a moment. Where does work pile up in your own system?

And back.

So now Erik has an ordered road, and he knows where it waits. But the code agent's next change is enormous. Two thousand lines. Should that worry him?

### Show notes

1. Goldratt and Cox, *The Goal* (1984) (theory): the constraint, Herbie, the five focusing steps: identify, exploit, subordinate, elevate, return to the first step and don't let inertia become the constraint.
2. Dettmer, *The Logical Thinking Process* (2007) (practice): the Prerequisite Tree ("in order to A, we must B"), the Future Reality Tree ("if cause, then effect"), injections and negative branches.
3. The tutor's links: the pile forms at the person's review; ordered objectives as a plan an agent reports against; an agent reading a step forward.

## Episode 5: How big a step

### Script

Previously: Erik found the constraint, and it's him, reviewing. Today his code agent hands him a change of two thousand lines.

There's research on this, and it's a different kind from what we've had so far. Nicole Forsgren and her colleagues at DORA have surveyed software teams for years. One of their long-standing findings is that working in small batches, changes small enough to test and judge quickly, goes with better software delivery. These are surveys, so they show that things go together, not that one causes the other.

With AI, the finding gets sharper. In DORA's twenty twenty-four report, each twenty-five percent increase in AI adoption went with an estimated seven point two percent drop in delivery stability. The authors think the likely reason is that AI makes it easy to produce larger changes. In twenty twenty-five, surveying nearly five thousand people, they found that AI's benefits depended on small batches, and they state that with a high degree of certainty.

My reading: the size of a step is how fast the road gives you feedback. A two-thousand-line change gives Erik one answer, late. Ten small ones give him ten, early.

So he sends it back, asking for smaller pieces. And his evenings get shorter. That one he got right.

Next, Dettmer again, with something he calls the logical glue that holds any reasoning together. They're questions you can ask of any argument, yours or someone else's. Is this actually true? Does this cause really lead to that effect? Could something else explain it? There are eight in all. My reading: a person and an agent can question each other's reasoning by the same rules. When the review agent says a change is safe, Erik can ask it: is that cause enough on its own?

Dettmer has two more trees, one for tracing today's troubles back to a core problem, and one for dissolving a conflict by finding the hidden assumption behind it. Both are for the desk, when you need them.

Take a moment. What's the largest step you've taken lately, with an agent? How long before you knew whether it worked?

And back.

Erik's road is in order now, the steps are small, and he can question the agents' reasoning. But questioning them takes him. Every answer they give, he has to read, judge, decide. His road is clearer, and his evenings are still his.

He doesn't walk this road alone. So what makes an agent a teammate, rather than one more thing to watch?

### Show notes

1. Forsgren, Humble and Kim, *Accelerate* (2018) (study): small batches among the capabilities that predict delivery performance.
2. DORA, *Accelerate State of DevOps Report 2024* (study: survey): each 25% more AI adoption, an estimated 7.2% less delivery stability. DORA, *State of AI-assisted Software Development 2025* (study: survey of nearly 5,000): AI's benefits depended on small batches, "with a high degree of certainty".
3. Dettmer's eight Categories of Legitimate Reservation (practice): clarity, entity existence, causality existence, cause sufficiency, additional cause, cause-effect reversal, predicted effect existence, tautology. The Current Reality Tree and the Evaporating Cloud (textbook module 3, concept 6).

## Episode 6: Teammates

### Script

Previously: Erik's road is in order and his steps are small. But every step still passes through him.

One morning his review agent approves a change, confidently. It says the tests pass and the design is consistent. Erik ships it. It breaks the sign-up page. Later he finds out why: the review agent had been working from last week's picture of the project. It didn't know the sign-up page had changed.

Gary Klein, who studies how experts decide in the field, and David Woods, who studies how people work with automation, have a name for what went wrong. Working together, they say, rests on two things: an agreement to work toward shared goals, and common ground, meaning what each party knows the other knows. And common ground isn't set once. It drifts as the work goes on, and has to be repaired. Erik's agent hadn't failed at reviewing. Its common ground with him had drifted.

Klein, Woods and colleagues list ten challenges for making automation a team player. Three stand out for Erik. An agent should be predictable, so you can foresee what it does. Its intentions should be easy to read. And you should be able to steer it. Being capable isn't enough. A brilliant agent you can't read or steer is a poor teammate. This is a framework, from researchers in cognitive systems engineering.

Then why did Erik trust that confident approval? Kahneman, again, together with Klein, asked when a quick judgement can be trusted. Their answer: only where the environment is regular, and the judge has learned it through rapid, clear feedback. Confidence is no guide. My reading: the same question applies to a model's fluent, confident output.

And here is the most direct evidence in the whole program on Erik's nagging question.

In twenty twenty-five, a group called METR ran a randomised trial. Sixteen experienced open-source developers worked on two hundred and forty-six real tasks, some with AI tools and some without. With the tools, they took nineteen percent longer. And afterwards, they believed AI had made them twenty percent faster.

Slower, and sure they were faster. Because it's a trial, it can show cause. It's also small, sixteen people, with early twenty twenty-five models, so things may have changed since. But it says something hard to ignore: you can't tell by feel whether AI is helping you.

Take a moment with that. How do you know your agents are helping?

And back.

My reading, and it's an important one: Erik's attention and his system's ease depend on each other. An agent that shows its status, and can be read and steered, takes less of him. One that has to be watched takes more. In episode four, the pile formed at Erik's review. Now it has a name. Erik's attention is the constraint.

So he asks his review agent to state, every time, which version of the project it reviewed. The next drift is caught in a second.

But he's repeating himself. Every morning, the same reminders, the same rules, typed again. How does he make what must hold, actually hold?

### Show notes

1. Klein, Feltovich, Bradshaw and Woods (2005), "Common Ground and Coordination in Joint Activity" (theory).
2. Klein, Woods, Bradshaw, Hoffman and Feltovich (2004), "Ten Challenges for Making Automation a 'Team Player'" (theory): among them, predictable and able to predict others, intentions easy to read, directable, status obvious.
3. Kahneman and Klein (2009), "Conditions for intuitive expertise: a failure to disagree" (theory).
4. Becker, Rush, Barnes and Rein, METR (2025), arXiv 2507.09089 (trial; sixteen developers, early-2025 models): nineteen percent slower with AI, believed twenty percent faster.
5. The tutor's links: confidence and a model's output; attention as the constraint, a coupled system.

## Episode 7: Built around the model

### Script

Previously: Erik's agents are becoming teammates. But he's still the one keeping them in line, every morning, by hand.

His rules file has grown to four pages. He pastes it into every task. And the agents seem to follow it less, not more.

Anthropic, the company that makes Claude, gives advice from its own experience: a model has a limited attention budget. The aim is the smallest set of the most useful information that makes the result you want likely. More context isn't better. That's practice: a vendor's advice. But Erik cuts his four pages to one, and the agents follow it better. He got that one right.

Now, what would let an agent check its own work? Here there's a benchmark. Researchers Mathews and Nagappan gave models programming problems, some with tests included. With tests, models solved twelve percent more problems on one set, and eight and a half percent more on another. Feeding failing tests back to them added about three percent more. A benchmark shows what models do on a fixed set of tasks, not more than that.

What about people? Here the evidence goes soft, and it's worth hearing. A meta-analysis of twenty-seven studies of test-driven development found a small gain in quality and little or none in productivity. And a study of thirty-nine professionals found the gain came from working in short, steady cycles. Whether the test was written first made no real difference. So "always write the test first" isn't what the evidence says, for people. It says: small, steady steps. For agents, my reading is that a test is a specification they can run, the rapid, clear feedback Kahneman and Klein asked for.

Birgitta Böckeler, a practitioner, gives a way to think about the rest. Build a harness around the model, she says, with two parts. Guides steer the agent before it acts. Sensors let it correct itself afterwards. Some are run as code: tests, linters, type checkers. Some are read and judged by a model: a rules file, an AI review. The aim is a loop that catches as much as possible before it ever reaches a person, though she adds that the person needn't be removed.

How far should Erik go? Alexey Krivitsky describes a ladder, from prompting and planning, up through verifying and enforcing, to orchestrating and supervising. His advice: climb only as high as your work demands. Anthropic says: do the simplest thing that works. Erik is tempted by the top rung. He doesn't take it.

These are all practitioners, and they agree with each other. Agreement isn't evidence. The tests research is the only evidence in this chapter.

My reading, back to the line in the sand: a rule you give an agent is a constraint. Some are governing, limits the harness enforces. Some are enabling, ways for the agent to act well in context.

Take a moment. Which rule have you told an agent more than once? Could the system hold it instead?

And back.

So Erik's rules hold now, without him. The tests run. The agents check themselves. And his system has become big enough that he can no longer see it. Where does it all stand?

### Show notes

1. Anthropic, "Effective context engineering for AI agents" (practice).
2. Mathews and Nagappan (2024), "Test-Driven Development for Code Generation", arXiv 2402.13521 (benchmark).
3. Rafique and Mišić (2013), meta-analysis of test-driven development (review; abstract only). Fucci and colleagues (2017), "A Dissection of the Test-Driven Development Process" (study).
4. Böckeler (2026), "Harness engineering for coding agent users"; Krivitsky, "Professional Agentic Product Engineering" (practice).
5. The tutor's links: a test as a runnable specification; rules as constraints.

## Episode 8: At a glance

### Script

Previously: Erik's rules hold without him. But his system has outgrown his view of it.

Each morning, he opens a status page. It's a wall of text: every task, every agent's log, every comment, in one long scroll. He reads for twenty minutes and still can't say where things stand.

Ben Shneiderman, a pioneer of how people see information on screens, put the answer in a sentence he calls a mantra: overview first, zoom and filter, then details on demand. See the whole. Narrow to what interests you. Only then open the detail. He lists seven tasks a good view supports, overview, zoom and filter among them, seven in all.

Edward Tufte, who has spent a career on how to show data well, says what to take away. Most of a graphic's ink, he says, should carry data. Erase the rest. Decoration that carries nothing, or hides what matters, he calls chartjunk: borders, shading, heavy grids, three-D effects. And he says what to put side by side. Small multiples: the same small graphic, repeated, so the eye can compare across them.

His favourite example is a map by Charles Joseph Minard, of Napoleon's march on Moscow. A band, as wide as the army, runs along the route and back. It starts thick, and it shrinks, and shrinks, as the soldiers die. Underneath runs the temperature, falling. One picture, and you see the whole catastrophe. Tufte calls it probably the best statistical graphic ever drawn.

Both Shneiderman and Tufte offer frameworks, from design research and practice.

So Erik rebuilds his morning view. One screen. His targets across the top. Under each, a small strip per agent, the same shape repeated, showing what's done, what's waiting, and where it's waiting. The detail only when he clicks.

Take a moment. When you open your own system, what does your eye land on first? Is it what you most need to know?

And back.

And there, on the new view, something shows that the old wall of text had hidden. Almost every waiting strip is waiting on the same thing. On Erik.

### Show notes

1. Shneiderman (1996), "The Eyes Have It" (theory): overview first, zoom and filter, then details on demand; the seven tasks, overview, zoom, filter, details on demand, relate, history, extract.
2. Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information* (1983) (theory): data-ink, chartjunk, small multiples, "above all else show the data"; his site on Minard's map (practice).
3. Erik's view is an illustration, not a finding.

## Episode 9: Staying well in it

### Script

Previously: Erik's new view showed him the whole system. And the thing almost everything waits on is him.

It's midnight, again. He's reading the agents' work, again. We met him here at the very start.

Goldratt returns with one last turn. The constraint, he said, isn't always a machine. It can be a less obvious resource, like skilled labour. A person. And exploiting a constraint means getting the most out of it as it is, not out of everything else.

My reading: in a builder's system, the constraint is often the builder's own attention. Getting the most out of it means not spending it on what an agent or a rule can carry. So Erik goes back over what he reads each night. Half of it, the harness now checks. A quarter, the agents can show him at a glance. What's left is what only he can judge.

Sarasvathy returns too. Remember the cupboards? Starting from what you have was half of it. She found more principles in expert entrepreneurs, and three of them matter here.

Affordable loss: at each step, decide what you're willing to lose, rather than chasing what you might gain. My reading: for Erik, losses come in tokens and in attention, and every probe carries one.

Lemonade: surprises are opportunities. When one of Erik's agents returns something unexpected, the question isn't how to get rid of it, but what it could be a lever for.

And the crazy quilt: build partnerships early, with people who commit. An agent commits nothing of its own. So Erik's quilt is made of people, those four who replied, now using his product and telling him what's missing.

Sarasvathy's principles are a framework, from her study of twenty-seven expert entrepreneurs, each thinking aloud through the same problems.

Klein adds one more practice. Before you start, imagine the plan has already failed. Everyone writes down why, alone, then shares one reason at a time. He calls it a premortem, and his point is that it makes it safe to name weaknesses. You may hear that it finds thirty percent more reasons. That figure comes from a single study in nineteen eighty-nine, and it counted reasons, not how good they were. The premortem is advice from experience, not a tested result. Erik asks one of his agents for its own list too, and compares.

Take a moment. What did the last month of building give you, and what did it take?

And back.

Now, the question from the start. Erik's nagging feeling: he couldn't say whether any of it was working.

Here's what the evidence says, and it's not comforting. The METR trial showed that experienced developers couldn't tell by feel whether AI was helping. They were slower and felt faster. Kahneman and Klein say why: confidence isn't a guide, only rapid, clear feedback is. So Erik's feeling was right in one way. He couldn't tell. Nobody can, by feel.

What he can do is build the feedback. And looking back, that's what each step was. A diagnosis that says what working means. An outside view that says what's realistic. Probes that show what lands. Small steps that report back early. Agents that show their state. Tests that check themselves. A view that shows the whole. And a builder who has stopped being the bottleneck for everything.

Erik still can't feel whether it works. But now he can see it.

That's the book. Everything you've heard is the beginning, not the landing. An idea becomes yours when you use it on your own work. That happens at the desk. When you're there, start with the first question, on your own target: working toward what?
